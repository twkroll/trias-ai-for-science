## 9. Conclusion

The descriptive Trias developed here is not a new triangle of reality, model, and computation. Its relational skeleton is strongly anticipated by the classical model-credibility tradition and by modern verification-and-validation practice. Nor does the paper introduce new categories of machine-learning success, provenance, identifiability, assurance, or scientific discovery. Its more limited proposal is to read target, theory, and computation as **claim-relative epistemic roles** and to localize evidence to the relation it directly supports.

This reinterpretation becomes useful when computational roles vary across AI-for-Science workflows. Predictive black-box models can support target-relative prediction without an explicit theory claim; simulator-trained surrogates make the distinction between synthetic and real referents explicit; physics-informed systems can support theory–computation, target–theory, and computation–target claims through different evidence; and equation discovery shows most clearly that a candidate theory can be an output of computation rather than a prior lifecycle stage. Classical controls and the project’s negative and inconclusive stress tests further show that the same vocabulary can preserve differences among supported, negative, uncertain, untested, and non-applicable claims without collapsing them into a global judgment of model quality.

The resulting contribution is therefore a compact, genealogically grounded **evidence-localization vocabulary**, not a replacement for specialist credibility frameworks. Its practical value for scientific communication, review, or decision-making remains untested. The central interpretive lesson is narrower: when a computational or AI system is said to work, the scientifically relevant question is what claim has actually been established, relative to which referent and scope, by which computational practice, and what additional conclusions still require independent evidence or an explicit bridge argument.
