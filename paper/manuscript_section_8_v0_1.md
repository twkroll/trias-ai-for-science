# Section 8 — Discussion: scientific success without global success

**Stand:** 2026-09-03  
**Depends on:** D035 / P3 / W1–W5 PASS

> **Manuscript note:** This discussion is intentionally descriptive. It does not propose a scalar model-quality score, a requirement to maximize all three relations, or a necessary trade-off law. Its purpose is to state the philosophical payoff of the role/evidence analysis while keeping practical usefulness as an open empirical question.

## 8. Discussion: scientific success without global success

The preceding cases suggest a simple but consequential way of reading claims that a computational or AI system “works.” Such language is often useful as shorthand, but it can be epistemically incomplete. A model may predict measured quantities accurately, reproduce a simulator almost exactly, satisfy an embedded physical constraint, preserve selected numerical structure, or recover an interpretable equation. Each of these can constitute a genuine scientific success. They need not, however, be successes of the same kind, and they do not support the same further conclusions. The descriptive Trias therefore treats success as claim-relative and relation-specific rather than as a single global property of a model.

This point should not be read as the discovery that science has multiple epistemic aims. Prediction, explanation, understanding, discovery, calibration, simulation, and decision support are already distinguished in philosophy of science, philosophy of machine learning, and scientific-ML methodology. The narrower proposal is to localize the evidence for those aims relative to a target or referent, a theory-level claim, and a computational practice. A low prediction error against held-out measurements can directly support a computation–target prediction claim. A low error against a simulator can instead support fidelity to a synthetic referent. A small physics residual can support a claim about how theoretical structure is realized computationally. A recovered symbolic equation introduces a theory-level object whose structural, dynamical, and empirical adequacy can then be assessed separately. In this sense, the question is not simply whether a model is successful, but what exactly has succeeded, relative to what, and with what evidence.

This interpretation also blocks a normative reading that would treat all three relations as dimensions to be maximized simultaneously. A black-box predictor need not be defective merely because no mechanistic theory claim is made. For a narrowly specified predictive use, `T = NONE_CLAIMED` can be the correct description rather than a low score. Conversely, an analytically elegant or mechanistically interpretable theory need not be operationally useful for every computational task, as the Sundman control illustrates. The role profile is therefore not an optimization objective and does not define an ideal model at the center of a triangle. `NOT_APPLICABLE` is not a penalty, and an untested relation is not automatically a failed one.

For the same reason, the Trias should not be interpreted as a necessary trade-off theory. The three relations can come into tension in particular scientific situations: increasing computational tractability may require approximations that weaken a theory-level claim; training on abundant synthetic data may improve simulator fidelity without establishing real-target validity; imposing a physical constraint may improve one form of structure preservation while reducing flexibility elsewhere. But none of these examples establishes a general zero-sum geometry. There is no claim that improving one relation must worsen another, nor that every scientific model occupies a Pareto frontier between reality, theory, and computation. In some cases strong evidence may be available across all relations relevant to the scientific use. In others, one relation may simply be outside the stated claim. The descriptive content is weaker and more defensible: evidence for one relation does not, without an additional argument, settle the others.

The phrase “without global success” should therefore be understood carefully. It does not imply that global assessments are impossible or illegitimate. Scientific projects routinely make higher-level judgments about whether a model is credible, useful, adequate for purpose, or ready for deployment. Such judgments are precisely where V&V, uncertainty quantification, assurance arguments, domain expertise, and decision analysis become indispensable. The Trias does not replace those judgments. It instead asks that the evidential ingredients of a broad success claim remain visible. A higher-level assessment may legitimately combine several relations, but the combination should not erase which claims are directly supported, which depend on bridge premises, and which remain untested or not applicable.

This is especially relevant to AI for Science because learned systems often compress several epistemic roles into one computational artifact. A single neural model may contain physical constraints, emulate a simulator, infer latent structure, and generate predictions that are compared with experiments. The resulting performance report can therefore mix evidence with different referents and different claim objects. The role-based reading does not solve the technical validation problem, but it provides a small translation layer across these activities. It makes it possible to say, for example, that a physics-informed model satisfies its encoded equations accurately while its empirical validity remains only partially tested, or that an equation-discovery system reproduces long-time dynamics while structural identification remains uncertain. The advantage is not a new error metric; it is the refusal to collapse these statements into a single model-quality label.

The project stress tests also show why this descriptive discipline matters for negative and inconclusive evidence. The Lorenz/SINDy study was technically capable of testing its preregistered structural-effect claim and did not satisfy the required seed-robust criterion. Its result is therefore informative negative evidence within that scope. The ML provenance study, by contrast, failed a learner-resolvability prerequisite by a large margin, so the downstream provenance claim was not decided. Calling both experiments “failures” would discard an important epistemic distinction. The same applies to `UNTESTED` and `NOT_APPLICABLE`: lack of evidence, inability to resolve a question, negative evidence, and absence of a claim are different states even when none licenses an affirmative conclusion.

The practical value of making these distinctions explicit remains an open question. The present paper has shown that the same compact vocabulary can describe classical numerical controls, predictive and hybrid AI workflows, synthetic surrogates, equation discovery, and negative or inconclusive project results without changing its core semantics. That establishes internal analytical coherence, not practical superiority. It has not been shown that scientists, reviewers, or interdisciplinary teams make better decisions when using the vocabulary, that disagreements are resolved more reliably, or that reporting becomes measurably clearer. Those are empirical hypotheses rather than conclusions of the present analysis.

Several limitations follow from the same modest positioning. First, assignments to `R`, `T`, and `C` are claim-relative and can be contestable. Data are theory-laden, computational models often contain multiple nested representations, and hybrid systems can blur the distinction between encoded theory and learned computation. The framework does not remove these interpretive problems; it forces them to be stated. Second, the three roles are intentionally coarse. Specialist frameworks should be used whenever finer distinctions are scientifically consequential. Third, the present synthesis is genealogical and selective rather than exhaustive: it connects model credibility, scientific ML, provenance, assurance reasoning, and inverse inference, but it does not subsume their technical content.

A natural next step would therefore be to test usefulness rather than to add further conceptual machinery. One option is a comparative case-study audit in which the same published AI-for-Science result is analyzed with and without an explicit role/evidence profile, asking whether the profile exposes ambiguities that affect interpretation. A second is an inter-rater or user study testing whether scientists from different domains agree more consistently about what a performance result warrants when the referent, theory status, computational role, and evidence relation are made explicit. A later project could also investigate normative extensions—for example, how different scientific aims should weight unresolved relations—but such an extension would be conceptually distinct from the descriptive proposal defended here.

The philosophical conclusion is therefore deliberately limited. Computational scientific success need not be a single all-or-nothing property, and an AI system need not satisfy every epistemic relation in order to be scientifically useful. What matters for interpretation is which success has actually been demonstrated. The descriptive Trias is intended to keep that localization visible: target-relative success, theory-related success, and computational fidelity can coexist, diverge, remain untested, or be inapplicable in different combinations. The scientific judgment comes afterwards, relative to the purpose of the inquiry and with the specialist methods appropriate to the claims at stake.
