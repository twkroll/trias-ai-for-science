## Recommended provisional final title

> **From Model Credibility to AI for Science: Claim-Relative Evidence Across Target, Theory, and Computation**

### Why this title is recommended

- It foregrounds the accepted genealogy rather than implying invention of a new triangle.
- `Claim-Relative Evidence` names the residual contribution more accurately than `framework` or `theory`.
- `Target, Theory, and Computation` preserves the three roles without requiring the project-specific word `Trias` in the title.
- `AI for Science` makes the intended domain explicit.

## Alternative titles

1. **Scientific Success Without Global Success: Target, Theory, and Computation in AI for Science**
2. **When an AI Model “Works”: Localizing Evidence Across Target, Theory, and Computation**
3. **From Model Credibility to AI for Science: A Descriptive Trias of Target, Theory, and Computation**

The first alternative emphasizes the Discussion but risks making `global success` appear stronger or more provocative than intended. The third retains project continuity but may suggest a more standalone framework than the manuscript ultimately claims. The recommended title therefore remains the most boundary-consistent.

## Abstract

Classical model-credibility methodology already distinguishes a problem entity or reality, a conceptual model, and a computerized model, together with corresponding questions of conceptual validity, verification, and operational validation. AI-for-Science workflows do not invalidate this structure, but they make its elements less natural as fixed lifecycle stages: computation may act as predictor, simulator surrogate, physics-constrained learner, or inference procedure that produces a candidate theory. We propose a descriptive, claim-relative reinterpretation in which `R` denotes the target or referent, `T` the theory-, mechanism-, or explanation-level content actually claimed, and `C` the concrete computational practice. Evidence is then localized to target–theory, theory–computation, or computation–target claims within a stated use and scope; support on one relation does not automatically establish the others without an explicit bridge argument. We apply the same vocabulary to predictive black-box models, synthetic surrogates, physics-informed learning, and equation discovery, and use classical numerical controls plus negative and inconclusive project results to test evidence-status discipline. The proposal does not replace V&V, provenance, assurance cases, identifiability, system identification, or Scientific-ML methodology. Its contribution is a modest cross-domain synthesis: a genealogically grounded evidence-localization vocabulary for making heterogeneous forms of computational scientific success comparable without reducing them to a single global model-quality label. Its practical usefulness remains to be tested.
