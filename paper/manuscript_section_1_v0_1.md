## 1. Introduction: when “the model works” is epistemically incomplete

Suppose two scientific machine-learning systems report the same low root-mean-square error. In the first case, the error is measured against independent observations of a physical system. In the second, it is measured against the outputs of a numerical simulator used as a training teacher. Numerically, the two results may look identical. Epistemically, they need not support the same claim. The first directly concerns performance relative to an empirical target; the second directly concerns fidelity to a synthetic or simulator-defined referent. Whether the second result can also support a claim about the physical system depends on additional premises about the simulator, the regime of use, and the size of the surrogate error. The phrase “the model works” therefore omits a scientifically important question: **what has been shown to work, relative to what, and for which claim?**

This question becomes increasingly visible in AI for Science because computation no longer occupies one uniform role. Learned systems may predict observations without making a mechanistic claim, emulate expensive numerical models, combine data with physical constraints, reconstruct partially observed states, or participate in the inference of candidate equations. These practices are individually well established. Philosophy of machine learning distinguishes prediction from explanation, discovery, understanding, and other epistemic aims; scientific-ML perspectives classify learned methods by the amount and kind of prior theoretical knowledge available; physics-informed learning combines data and governing structure; and equation-discovery methods use computation to produce interpretable model candidates (e.g. Karniadakis et al., 2021; Naser, 2025; Vinuesa et al., 2026; Kramer et al., 2026). The issue addressed here is not that these roles have gone unnoticed. It is that success in these different roles is easy to compress into global labels—accurate, validated, physics-informed, discovered—even though the supporting evidence can attach to different scientific relations.

There is also no novelty in introducing a triangle of reality, model, and computation. A direct historical ancestor is the model-credibility tradition associated with Schlesinger, Sargent, and later verification-and-validation practice. That tradition distinguishes a problem entity or reality, a conceptual model, and a computerized model, together with conceptual-model validity, computerized-model verification, and operational validity. Modern V&V and VVUQ frameworks further make model credibility dependent on intended use, quantities of interest, scope, uncertainty, sensitivity, and acceptance criteria. Recent Scientific-ML credibility work extends the same concerns to learned and hybrid models. **The triangle is not new.** Any contribution of the present paper must therefore lie elsewhere.

The proposal developed here is to reinterpret this classical structure as a set of **claim-relative epistemic roles** rather than as a fixed sequence of lifecycle stages. We use `R` for the target or referent of the claim, which may be real, synthetic, or hybrid; `T` for the theory-, mechanism-, structure-, or explanation-level content actually being asserted; and `C` for the concrete computational practice. In a conventional simulation, a theory may be specified before it is implemented computationally. In predictive black-box learning, no theory-level claim may be part of the stated scientific use. In equation discovery, a candidate `T` may instead be an output of `C`. In a surrogate workflow, one computational model can become the synthetic referent for another. The roles are therefore fixed by the claim under assessment, not by a permanent ontology of artifacts or by their chronological order.

This role-based reading preserves the classical distinction among target–theory, theory–computation, and computation–target questions while changing how they are used across heterogeneous workflows. Evidence is treated as relation-specific rather than as evidence for a single latent model quality. A low physics residual can support a claim about how theoretical structure is realized computationally; it does not by itself establish that the theory is adequate for the target or that the resulting model is empirically valid. A strong real-world prediction can support computation–target adequacy without uniquely identifying a mechanism. A surrogate can closely reproduce its teacher while the corresponding real-target claim remains untested. When evidence is intentionally carried from one relation to another, the additional premises should be made explicit as a bridge argument rather than assumed silently.

The central contribution is therefore deliberately modest. The paper proposes a **genealogically grounded evidence-localization vocabulary** for comparing heterogeneous computational scientific workflows. It does not replace verification and validation, provenance analysis, assurance cases, identifiability theory, system identification, or the philosophy and methodology of machine learning. Those frameworks provide the substantive technical and argumentative machinery. The role profile asks a more limited cross-case question: which target, which theory-level claim, and which computational practice are connected by the reported evidence, and what remains unsupported, uncertain, untested, or not applicable?

Four AI-for-Science configurations provide the main conceptual test: predictive black-box models, synthetic surrogates, physics-informed or hybrid learning, and equation discovery. The strongest contrasts are the referent switch between simulator fidelity and real-target validation, and the reversal in equation discovery where computation can produce a candidate theory-level object. Two classical controls—Sundman’s convergent but practically unwieldy three-body representation and a numerical figure-eight comparison—show where ordinary numerical analysis and V&V already suffice. Two project stress tests then preserve a further distinction that a global “works/fails” vocabulary can obscure: a preregistered Lorenz/SINDy study produced an `INFORMATIVE_NEGATIVE` result, whereas an ML-provenance study was `INCONCLUSIVE_LEARNER_ERROR` because the learner could not resolve the signal required to decide the target claim.

The resulting philosophical position is descriptive rather than normative. The paper does not propose a scalar quality score, an ideal point at which all three relations are maximized, or a necessary trade-off among target, theory, and computation. A prediction-only model can be scientifically useful without a mechanistic claim, and `NOT_APPLICABLE` is not a deficit. Conversely, strong evidence on one relation does not automatically settle the others. Higher-level judgments of credibility, adequacy for purpose, or readiness for use remain legitimate and depend on the specialist methods appropriate to the scientific task. The aim of the present synthesis is only to keep the evidential ingredients of those judgments visible.
